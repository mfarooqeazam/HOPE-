02 · Therapy

Home › Therapy

Therapy for your child

Four disciplines under one roof, planned together rather than in parallel. You do not need a diagnosis or a referral to start.

What we offer

Four kinds of therapy

Applied Behaviour Analysis

A structured, data-led approach to building communication, social and daily-living skills. We begin with a functional behaviour assessment to understand what a behaviour achieves for your child, then build a plan around it.

Often helps with: communication, following routines, managing transitions, self-care.

Speech & language therapy

Support for understanding and using language — spoken, gestural, or through communication aids. Communication is not only speech, and a child who cannot yet speak is not a child without something to say.

Often helps with: expressive and receptive language, articulation, social communication.

Occupational therapy

Work on the practical skills a day is made of — fine motor control, handwriting, dressing, feeding — and on sensory processing, where everyday input can feel overwhelming or insufficient.

Often helps with: fine motor skills, sensory regulation, independence in daily routines.

Physiotherapy

Movement, strength, balance and posture, worked on in ways that fit into ordinary life rather than staying in the treatment room.

Often helps with: gross motor skills, mobility, coordination, physical confidence.

What to expect

Your first session

The first appointment is an assessment, not a treatment session. We spend it understanding your child and hearing from you — you know things about your child that no assessment will surface.

  • We talk through your concerns and what a good outcome would look like for your family.
  • The relevant therapist works with your child — often through play, so it rarely feels like a test.
  • You are welcome to stay in the room. Many children settle faster that way.
  • We tell you honestly whether we are the right service. If we are not, we say so.

How we work

Principles behind the plan

Individualised goals

Targets are set for your child and reviewed as they change — never copied from a template.

Skills that generalise

A skill that only appears in the therapy room is not yet a skill. We plan for home and school.

Caregiver training

You are taught the strategies too. Family involvement is the subject of our founder's own research.

Data-led review

Progress is measured, so a plan that is not working gets changed rather than repeated.

One team

Therapists and teachers plan together, so goals reinforce rather than compete.

Honest expectations

We do not promise recovery or cure. We tell you what we can reasonably work towards.

An inclusive school, funded for families who need it

Neurodivergent and neurotypical children learning in the same classroom — because separating them teaches both groups the wrong lesson.

The model

How inclusion actually works here

Inclusion is not simply placing a disabled child in a mainstream room and hoping. It means the room is built so that more than one kind of learner belongs in it from the start.

  • Individualised education plans. Each child has goals of their own; the class does not move at one speed.
  • Therapy inside the school day. A skill worked on in the morning gets practised in the classroom the same week.
  • Staff trained in behaviour analysis. The teaching team is trained on site, to an international standard.
  • Both groups gain. Neurotypical children who grow up alongside disabled peers do not have to learn inclusion later as an abstraction.

Admissions

Places are funded rather than charged for, and awarded on need. What we establish first is whether the setting suits your child; the financial assessment is a separate, private conversation.

Why starting sooner matters, and why a number of places here are funded rather than charged for. Neither is a sales argument — they are the two facts that shaped how this centre works.

Why now — a note on waiting

A year of waiting is not a year paused

This is the part most families are never told plainly. Development does not hold still while a child waits for a place, a diagnosis, or the money. It carries on — just along a different path.

1 in 31 Children identified with autism in the United States in 2022 — up from 1 in 150 in 2000. This is not a rare condition. It is a common one that most systems are still built to treat as rare. CDC, ADDM Network — published April 2025 1
Age 2 Autism can be reliably identified by a trained clinician from around this age. In practice most children are diagnosed years later — and every one of those years is a year of support not given. Consistent finding across ADDM surveillance 1
21% Of children who left ABA within two years had actually met their goals. The other four in five stopped for reasons that had nothing to do with whether it was working. Choi et al., J Dev Behav Pediatr, 2022 2

What a delay actually costs

Nothing dramatic happens on the day you decide to wait. The cost is quiet, and it accumulates.

  • Skills compound, and so does their absence. A child who cannot yet ask for what they want does not simply wait to learn — they find another way to be understood, and that way is usually harder to live with for everyone.
  • Behaviour hardens into habit. What starts as frustration becomes a pattern, and a pattern that has worked for three years takes longer to change than one that is three months old.
  • School gets harder to enter, not easier. The gap between a child and their classroom widens with time, and each year of it makes an inclusive placement more difficult to arrange.
  • The family pays too. Parenting stress rises with unmet need, and a stressed household is a harder place to practise new skills — which is precisely what makes them stick.

And the part that should give you hope

The same study that measured how many families drop out also measured what happened to those who stayed.

The children who completed two years made clinically significant gains — including the children who began with the greatest needs.

Read that alongside the 21%. Families are not losing therapy because it failed them. They are losing it because they run out of money, or time, or hope, before it has finished working. Which of those three we can help with, we will.

We do not promise recovery or cure, and you should be wary of anyone who does. What we can say is that starting sooner gives us more to work with, and that the first conversation costs nothing.

Therapy is not a luxury. It is only priced like one.

In Islamabad a therapy session starts at around PKR 1,500 and runs past PKR 3,000. A child who needs intensive support does not need one session a week — many need five or six.

A study of 334 children referred for ABA found that 13% never started, a third had stopped within a year, and more than half were gone by two years — but only 21% of those departures happened because the child had met their goals. Volunteer with us

Mental health work, wherever we can be useful — with children, with adults, and in the places most services never reach. Someone still does the work. A good deal of it is done by volunteers.

Register as a volunteer Partner as a company

Four ways to help

There is a role here whatever you bring

You do not need a clinical background, and you do not need to know anything about autism. The work spans mental health broadly — children, adults, older people. Some of the most useful volunteers have never worked in this field at all — they can drive, cook, photograph, fix, teach a craft, or simply sit with someone.

Individuals

Help at camps and open days, assist in the classroom, prepare learning materials, or spend time with an adult who has few visitors. Regular volunteers are matched to one group so the people they support see the same face each week.

Students & professionals

Psychology and education students, therapists, teachers, SLPs and OTs. Volunteering here can run alongside supervised hours — if you are working towards IBA or IBT certification, say so and we will structure it properly rather than informally.

Companies & institutions

Team volunteering days, CSR partnerships, sponsoring a therapy place or a classroom's materials, donating equipment, or offering skills your staff already have — accounting, design, transport, IT. We will tell you honestly which of those we actually need this quarter.

From a distance

You do not have to be in Islamabad. Translation, admin, fundraising, social media, grant writing and remote mentoring all get done by people we have never met in person.

Not only children, not only autism

Mental health, wherever we can be useful

The centre began with children, but the founder is a clinical psychologist and the need does not stop at childhood or at one diagnosis. We work with adults as well — and we take on mental health work anywhere we can genuinely help, not only autism.

Autistic children become autistic adults, and almost every service in the country stops at eighteen. Alongside that: older adults who are isolated, carers who are exhausted, adults living with anxiety, depression or grief, and people whose disability was never assessed at all.

Volunteers are needed for all of it — independence and daily routines, employment readiness, community access, and simple company, which for isolated adults is the thing in shortest supply. This is often where someone with ordinary life experience is worth more than a clinician. If you can teach a person to cook, use a bus, sit an interview or hold a conversation, you are qualified.

Where it all began

The Project Hope was founded as a multidisciplinary centre for children with special needs in Islamabad, and now trains and consults worldwide.

Our story

I kept meeting parents who had run out of places to ask

Before this was a centre, it was a corridor. Hospital waiting areas at Shifa, then classrooms at Beaconhouse, and in all of them the same family, over and over — a mother holding a folder of reports she had been handed by four different people, none of whom had told her what to do on Monday morning.

What I saw was not grief about a diagnosis. Most of these parents had made their peace with the word. What was breaking them was the not-knowing: no path, no order to the steps, no one saying start here, then this. They were doing everything asked of them and still watching months pass. And when a plan finally did exist, it came with a fee that ran past what the household earned, so it quietly became a plan for someone else's child.

You can watch a parent decide to stop hoping. It is not dramatic. They stop asking questions in the session. They start saying we will see instead of what is next. They cancel one appointment and do not rebook, because rebooking means saying out loud that the money is gone. That is the moment I could not walk past any more.

So I went and measured it. My clinical psychology thesis asked whether a caregiver's stress changes what a child actually keeps from therapy — and it does. An exhausted, unsupported parent cannot carry a plan into the home, and a skill that never leaves the therapy room was never really learned. Which means supporting the parent is not kindness around the edges of the work. It is the work.

The Project Hope opened in August 2025 for exactly that reason. Not to promise recovery, and not to be one more place that hands a family a report. To be the place that says: here is the path, here is who walks it with you, and here is what happens if you cannot pay — because that answer should never be nothing.

Hope is not a feeling you hand to a family. It is a path you show them, and then walk with them.

Muhammad Farooq E Azam Awan — Founder, clinical psychologist & IBAO-certified Behavior Analyst
