The model

How inclusion actually works here

Inclusion is not simply placing a disabled child in a mainstream room and hoping. It means the room is built so that more than one kind of learner belongs in it from the start.

  • Individualised education plans. Each child has goals of their own; the class does not move at one speed.
  • Therapy inside the school day. A skill worked on in the morning gets practised in the classroom the same week.
  • Staff trained in behaviour analysis. The teaching team is trained on site, to an international standard.
  • Both groups gain. Neurotypical children who grow up alongside disabled peers do not have to learn inclusion later as an abstraction.

Why we give it away

Therapy is not a luxury. It is only priced like one.

Neurodevelopmental conditions are not rare, and the therapies that help are not optional extras. For a child who cannot yet speak, sit in a classroom, or manage a morning, therapy is the difference between a life with a door open and one without. Almost everywhere in the world, it costs more than the families who need it most can pay.

What that looks like in Pakistan

Pakistan has never run a national prevalence study. The Pakistan Autism Society estimates 350,000 to 400,000 children; published research is city-specific and does not agree. Nobody knows the real number — and that is not a footnote, it is the first problem. A child who is never counted is never planned for.

What we do know is what families here actually pay. In Islamabad a therapy session starts at around PKR 1,500 and runs past PKR 3,000. And a child who needs intensive support does not need one session a week. Many need five or six.

PKR 30,000 A month of therapy at the cheapest end — five sessions a week at PKR 1,500. 81% of a full minimum-wage income
PKR 72,000 A month at six sessions a week at PKR 3,000. Common for a child with high support needs, not extreme. 195% — nearly twice a minimum-wage income
PKR 37,000 The monthly minimum wage in Islamabad. The whole household income, for many families. ICT, financial year 2025–26

Read those three numbers together, because that is how a parent reads them. At the lowest possible rate, therapy for one child costs four-fifths of an entire minimum-wage income. At the rate many children actually need, it costs roughly twice that income — and the household still has rent, food, and any other children.

None of it includes transport five or six days a week, the initial assessment, school, or the equipment some children need. Nor does it count the parent who gives up paid work to make the schedule possible, which is often the real cost and never appears on an invoice.

Those figures are calculated at four weeks to a month. The real month is longer.

Why families stop

No parent decides to give up on their child. What actually happens is quieter than that. The sessions get spaced further apart to make the month work. One is missed, then two. The follow-up call is not returned, because returning it means saying out loud that the money has run out.

This is measurable, and it has been measured. A study of 334 children referred for ABA in a large Californian health system found that 13% never started at all. A third had stopped within a year. By two years, more than half were gone — and only 21% of those departures happened because the child had met their goals. The rest left for reasons that had nothing to do with whether the therapy was working: money, insurance, moving, exhaustion.

That is what happens in a wealthy country, with insurance, under a law requiring that insurance to cover it. Now put a family in Islamabad in the same position, paying twice the minimum wage each month out of pocket, with no insurance to lose because there was never any. The arithmetic above does not permit two years. For a household at or near the minimum wage, it does not permit two months.

The hardest part is what the same study found next: the children who did stay the full two years made clinically significant gains — including the children who started with the greatest needs. The therapy works. Families are not losing it because it failed them. They are losing it because they ran out of money before it had time to finish.

That is the gap this centre exists to close. Not to prove a point about funding, but because a child’s progress should not depend on whether their parents can sustain a monthly fee for two years.

Why it is free

Nobody should choose between rent and their child's education

Specialist education is expensive almost everywhere, and in practice that means the children who most need it are the ones least likely to get it. Running the school free of charge is a deliberate answer to that.
